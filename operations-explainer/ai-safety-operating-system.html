AI safety recruiters often talk about giving operations people context on AI safety. This page does the reverse. It gives people in AI safety context on operations: what the profession covers, where its edges are, and how its meaning shifts from one kind of organisation to another.

Knowing what an organisation should do is different from having the capability to do it consistently, at scale and under pressure. Operations is the discipline that builds that capability, and it is what turns research, policy ideas and good intentions into results.

Operations is not one job. In a five-person research group it can mean everything that is not the research. In a large company or a government department it usually means the opposite end of the spectrum: the people who deliver the service to customers, while finance, HR and IT are separate professions. Both uses are correct. They describe different parts of the same field.

What connects them is a way of working. Operations people care about whether the thing happens in the real world, at the quality and cost intended, and whether the organisation is set up so that it keeps happening. They build the conditions for success and they see delivery through. That is why a good operations hire multiplies the output of everyone around them.

What operations is

The discipline of designing, building, running and improving how an organisation works

Operations people ask the same four questions of any commitment an organisation makes. The loop never finishes, because the organisation and its risks keep changing.

  1. DesignDecide how the work should flow, who decides what, and what good looks like.
  2. BuildPut the people, systems, contracts and budgets in place to make that design real.
  3. ExecuteRun it every day, at the quality intended, including on the bad days.
  4. ImproveMeasure what happened, learn from it, and change the design.

Timing

Embedding operations early

Many founders bring in operations once something has started to hurt. By then, habits, contracts and funding terms have often formed around the gaps, and fixing them means unpicking what is already there. Building some operational foundations early is usually cheaper. It does not require a large team. It requires a few deliberate choices.

  • StructureClear owners, decision rights and a regular planning rhythm, so people know who decides and what matters now.
  • Foundations for growthFinance, people, legal and systems set up so they can take much more volume without being rebuilt.
  • Delivery and value focusWork planned around the outcomes that matter most, with honest measures of whether they are happening.
  • Continuous improvementA habit of reviewing what happened and changing how things work as a result.

What it changes

Researchers and founders spend more of their time on the work only they can do. Decisions are quicker because the routes and owners already exist. Growth adds capacity rather than confusion, because new people join systems that work. Funders, partners and governments find it easier to rely on the organisation because it can show what it delivers.

Early operations should be proportionate. Too much process too soon slows a small team down. The aim is the minimum structure the next stage of growth will need, reviewed as the organisation changes.

What the AI safety examples draw on

  • 80,000 Hours, "Scaling organisations making AI go well". Reports that COOs, chiefs of staff, operations directors and senior programme and people managers are among the hardest roles for AI safety organisations to fill, and notes that work like payroll and tax is usually delegated to staff or consultants.
  • MATS, Operations Generalist. Fellow and mentor support, events, office and programme logistics, internal systems and automation, office security procedures, with housing, people ops, finance and IT as growth areas.
  • BlueDot Impact, Head of Operations (read through a job aggregator, so it may be out of date). Scaling the team, people ops, managing accountants and lawyers, disbursing grants to hundreds of people across countries, setting up a new office, and coordinating visas and accommodation for a residency programme.
  • Center for AI Safety, Operations Associate (aggregator listing). Office and facilities, IT and systems administration, people operations and team support.
  • Apollo Research, operations expression of interest (aggregator listing). Generalist people operations: hiring, onboarding, HR systems, and using AI tools to cut manual work.

Assumptions to check

  • The five examples in the In practice tab come from the brief for this version. The spans under each, and which operational elements carry them, are a first draft.
  • The connections in the system diagram are illustrative. They show the kind of dependency that exists, and are not a complete list.
  • Marketing and investor relations were added in this version. Investor relations is written to cover both investors in companies and major funders of non-profits.
  • The ownership ratings for each organisation type are judgement calls based on common practice, not survey data. They show the typical pattern and real organisations vary.
  • Research operations (compute, model access, research project management) is included from general knowledge of the field. The job adverts read for this draft did not describe it in detail.
  • The public-sector end of the map (front-line delivery, investigation and counter-fraud, policy implementation) is written generically. It has not been checked against any individual's career history.
  • The six organisation types are a starting set. Frontier AI labs are grouped with large companies.
  • The hiring guide's backgrounds and interview questions are suggestions, not validated predictors.
